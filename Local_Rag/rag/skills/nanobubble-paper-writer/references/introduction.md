# Introduction

The Introduction is an argument that funnels from broad context down to a precise gap and a clearly stated aim. It promises what the paper will deliver, and nothing it promises may exceed what the Results and Discussion actually establish.

House style, cohesion rules, and the anti-tell catalogue in `house-style.md` apply throughout.
Attested frames for every move below are in `phrase-bank.md` §2.

## Corpus anchors (measured from the reference Introductions)

| Property | Rate per 1,000 words | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Citations `[n]` | **7.12** | The highest of any section by a wide margin — roughly one citation every seven words of running text. An Introduction paragraph without citations is almost certainly an unsupported claim. |
| `However` | **1.87** | Also the highest of any section. The Introduction is where tension is staged. |
| Hedges (`may`/`might`/`could`) | 2.05 | Prior work is reported as claims made, not facts settled. |
| `we` / `our` | 2.96 | Present but restrained; the aim statement is where it concentrates. |
| Passive | 2.66 | Low. Introductions are active: *X et al. showed*, not *it was shown*. |
| Sentence length | median 24 words, p90 43 | The longest sentences in the paper live here, carrying layered background. |

The single most transferable finding: attribution in this corpus is always **specific** — a named
group, a numbered citation, and the actual finding ("Recently, Tan et al. [7] reported a model
accounting for the long lifetime of bulk nanobubbles based on experimental studies."). Vague
attribution (*studies show*, *research suggests*, *it is widely believed*) does not occur. If you
cannot name who, do not make the claim.

## The structure: the moves

The invariant is the **funnel**: broad context → narrowing background → a tension or open question → the gap → the aim of the present work. The opening move can take several forms; pick whichever fits the material.

1. **Orientation.** Situate the topic in one of these ways: state directly what the study does ("The present study is devoted to..."); or lead with applications and why the topic matters (in nanobubble work: water treatment, flotation, surface cleaning, ultrasound contrast, agriculture); or ground it historically ("Long-lived sub-micron gas bodies in water were first inferred from..."); or open from a foundational fact the reader accepts. Keep it specific; no generic throat-clearing.

2. **Background / physical picture.** Build the conceptual ground in layers so a non-specialist in the subfield can follow. Define every term and symbol on first use. Integrate governing relations by name and, where used, by number ("according to the Young–Laplace equation...", "Equation (1) implies..."). In nanobubble papers this move typically establishes the Laplace pressure and the diffusion-driven dissolution it implies.

3. **Tension, paradox, or open question.** Surface the problem that motivates the work. The nanobubble field is built on exactly such a tension: classical theory predicts microsecond-scale dissolution, yet the objects persist for hours to weeks. A contradiction between prediction and observation, a long-standing controversy, or a naive expectation that fails ("One would expect... It is, however, by no means obvious that...") all serve.

4. **Literature-grounded review.** Lay out the rival ideas or the chain of prior results, densely cited. Use explicit scaffolding when there are parallel items: "In the first scenario... In the second scenario...", or "It was first shown that..., and subsequently that...". Attribute fairly; distinguish what is established from what is contested. In this field that distinction is load-bearing (see `nanobubble-domain.md`).

5. **The gap.** Name precisely what remains untested, scarce, unresolved, or debated, and why it matters. This is the hinge of the whole introduction, and it must point straight at the aim. A gap is not "little is known about X"; it is "measurements of X under condition Y, which would discriminate between mechanisms A and B, have not been reported".

6. **Aim / contribution.** State it explicitly with a canonical opener ("The present study is devoted to...", "In this work, we...", "The principal aim of this paper is to...", "Here we report..."). Name the approach and the headline result or claim, calibrated to what the paper actually shows.

7. **Roadmap (optional, common in longer papers).** "The remainder of this article is organized as follows..." with one clause per section.

Not every introduction uses all seven, and the order of 1–4 can vary, but moves 5 and 6 (gap, then aim) must always be present and must come near the end.

## Craft notes specific to the Introduction

- **Certainty calibration is at its most visible here.** Settled physics is asserted ("The excess pressure inside a bubble of radius R is 2γ/R"); contested interpretations are attributed and hedged ("it has been argued that...", "an alternative view holds that...").
- **Citation density is highest here.** Every non-obvious claim carries its reference, attached to the specific claim it supports; grouped references use ranges [12–17]; pointer forms are available ("for a review, see Ref. [n]").
- **The aim sentence is a contract.** Write it last, after the Results and Discussion exist or are known, and check it against them. Overclaiming in the aim is the most common referee complaint traceable to the Introduction.
- **Length.** Typically 3–6 paragraphs for a research paper; do not let the literature review (move 4) swell into a mini-review unless the venue expects it.

## Checklist (in addition to the global one)

- [ ] Funnel intact: opens broad, narrows to a specific gap, lands on the aim.
- [ ] The gap is precise and points directly at the aim.
- [ ] Explicit aim statement near the end, using a canonical opener, calibrated to the actual findings.
- [ ] Every governing equation or law introduced by name; symbols defined at first appearance.
- [ ] Settled vs. contested clearly separated, with the field's live disputes represented fairly.
- [ ] Citation attached to every non-obvious claim.
