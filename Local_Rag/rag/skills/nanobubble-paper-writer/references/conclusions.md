# Conclusions (and Abstract, and Title)

## Conclusions

The Conclusions section settles the account. It states what the paper established, at exactly the strength the evidence supports, and points forward briefly. It is short (typically one to three paragraphs), contains nothing new, and is often read immediately after the Abstract by a skimming reader, so it must stand alone. Attested frames are in `phrase-bank.md` §7; Abstract frames are in §8.

One further corpus habit worth adopting: `In summary` is used **mid-paper**, to close a long Results
subsection before moving on, not only at the head of the Conclusions. It is under-used in most
drafts.

### Structure

1. **What was done**, in one or two sentences: system, approach, scope. ("Bulk nanobubbles were generated in ultrapure water by acoustic cavitation and characterized over N weeks by NTA, DLS, and zeta-potential measurements, together with degassed, filtered, and solvent controls.")
2. **What was established**, as a short series of findings in descending order of importance, each stated with its number where the number is the finding. Prose, not bullets, unless the journal convention uses numbered conclusions.
3. **What it means**, in one or two sentences: the answer to the gap named in the Introduction, and the strongest fair claim about mechanism or identity the evidence supports.
4. **What comes next** (optional, one or two sentences): the specific experiment or measurement that would resolve what this work could not. Specific ("direct compositional probing of individual sub-200 nm entities") beats generic ("further work is needed", which is banned). The house corpus pattern is a short series of concrete verbs with concrete objects: "future work should evaluate X under pressurized flow-through conditions, assess pore-scale displacement behavior in representative core material, and quantify Y in the presence of Z."

### Craft notes

- **Symmetry with the aim.** The Conclusions must answer the Introduction's aim sentence claim for claim. If the aim promised a discrimination between two mechanisms and the data only constrained one, the Conclusions say so.
- **No new results, citations (rarely needed), figures, or arguments.** If a point needs defending, it is defended in the Discussion.
- **Calibration is at its most exposed here** because these sentences are the ones quoted. "The results combined provide strong evidence that..." and "we estimate that..." are the register of careful workers in this field; "we have proven definitively" is the register that draws published rebuttals.
- **Do not clone the Abstract.** The Abstract compresses the whole paper including motivation and methods; the Conclusions weigh outcomes. If the two read identically, rewrite the Conclusions to spend fewer words on setup and more on the standing of each claim.
- Banned closers: "further research is needed" (say which research), "the future looks bright", any sentence about the field rather than the findings.

### Checklist

- [ ] Stands alone; a reader of only this section knows what was done, found, and claimed.
- [ ] Answers the Introduction's aim claim for claim.
- [ ] Findings ordered by importance and carry their numbers.
- [ ] Claim strength exactly matches the evidence; contested-field humility preserved.
- [ ] Nothing new; no clone of the Abstract; no banned closers.

## Abstract

Written last. One paragraph (or the journal's structured format), typically 150–250 words, four moves in order:

1. **Context and problem** (1–2 sentences): the tension or gap, specific, no throat-clearing. In nanobubble work, one sentence usually suffices because the paradox is famous.
2. **What was done** (1–2 sentences): system, method, scope, with the key parameters.
3. **What was found** (2–4 sentences): the principal results with their numbers. The abstract's credibility lives in these digits ("mean diameter 150 ± 10 nm, number densities up to 10^8 mL^-1, stable for at least 30 days").
4. **What it means** (1 sentence): the strongest fair conclusion.

No citations, no undefined abbreviations, no sentence that could open any paper in the field. Every abstract sentence must be falsifiable by reading the paper.

## Title

- Prefer a declarative or precisely descriptive title over a cute one: the finding or the system-plus-question. "Long-term stability of acoustically generated bulk nanobubbles in ultrapure water" tells a searcher exactly what is inside.
- A declarative-claim title ("Bulk nanobubbles generated by acoustic cavitation are gas-filled") is legitimate when the paper genuinely establishes the claim; do not use one for a contested conclusion.
- No colon-riddled double titles unless the venue's culture expects them; no puns in a field with a live credibility dispute; include the terms searchers use (bulk/surface nanobubbles, stability, the generation method).
